The Boiler Problem · Scale Formation

Hardness & boiler scale

Raw feed water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium minerals. When heated inside a steam boiler, these soluble salts decompose and precipitate into rock-hard insulating scale.

    Why It Happens

    Boiling breaks unstable bicarbonates into insoluble CaCO₃ crystals that deposit onto the heating surface.

    The Industrial Hazard

    Scale insulates water from metal walls. Metal overheats rapidly beyond safety limits, leading to bulging and tube rupture.

    Key point for exams: Distinguish clearly between sludge and scale. Sludge is soft, slimy, non-adherent, and forms at cooler zones (flushed out by blow-down). Scale is hard, crystalline, tightly adherent, and forms on the hottest heating surfaces. Remind students why all hardness is expressed in terms of CaCO3 equivalent—because its molecular weight is exactly 100 and equivalent weight is 50, which simplifies stoichiometric calculations. Run the simulation to show how thermal decomposition of bicarbonate releases CO2 gas and precipitates insoluble CaCO3 crust.

    External Treatment · Cation Softener

    Zeolite softening process

    Hard water filters through a porous bed of sodium aluminosilicate (Na₂Ze). The active bed captures hard Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions and releases harmless Na⁺ ions into the soft effluent.

      Cation Exchange Swap

      Each divalent Ca²⁺ or Mg²⁺ ion captured in the lattice displaces exactly two monovalent Na⁺ ions into the soft water.

      10% Brine Regeneration

      When the bed exhausts, flushing with 10% NaCl salt water forces the equilibrium backwards, restoring active Na₂Ze for reuse.

      Emphasize charge stoichiometry: exactly 2 Na+ ions leave for every 1 Ca2+ or Mg2+ captured to preserve net zero electrical charge. When explaining regeneration, mention Le Chatelier's principle—the huge concentration gradient of 10% sodium chloride forces the reversible reaction backwards. Crucial viva point: why zeolite water can cause caustic embrittlement in high-pressure boilers (NaHCO3 decomposes into NaOH, which attacks stressed rivet joints).

      External Treatment · Pure Water

      Ion-exchange demineralisation

      High-pressure boilers require water completely free of all mineral salts. Water flows through two resin beds in series to eliminate both cations and anions, producing ultra-pure DM water.

        Two-Column Removal

        Column 1 (Cation) swaps metal ions for H⁺. Column 2 (Anion) swaps acid ions for OH⁻. The released H⁺ and OH⁻ combine into pure H₂O.

        Why Cation First

        Passing raw water into the anion bed first would make it strongly alkaline, instantly choking the resin pores with insoluble hydroxide sludge.

        Walk through the two columns in order: Cation bed releases H+, Anion bed releases OH-, and they neutralize into neutral H2O. Point to the live meter transitioning from PURE to IONS during exhaustion. Remind students that Ion Exchange is the ONLY method among these three that removes all dissolved salts, making it suitable for modern high-pressure boilers. Mention that the cation bed is regenerated with dilute HCl, and the anion bed with dilute NaOH.

        Membrane Desalination · Brackish Water

        Electrodialysis desalter

        A direct-current (DC) electric field pulls dissolved salt ions across alternating ion-selective plastic membranes, separating brackish feed into desalted water and reject brine.

          Selective Barriers

          Cation membranes (CM) permit only positive ions toward the cathode (−). Anion membranes (AM) permit only negative ions toward the anode (+).

          Continuous Dual Streams

          Alternate compartments collect fresh desalted water while adjacent chambers collect reject brine. Runs purely on DC electrical power.

          Highlight that Electrodialysis is an electrochemical membrane process, not an ion exchange bed. It does not require acids or alkalis for regeneration, but it consumes DC electrical power. Alternate chambers become concentrated brine and dilute product water. Don't forget the limitation: non-ionic impurities like bacteria and organic colloids are unaffected by the electric field.

          Membrane Separation · External Treatment

          Reverse osmosis (RO)

          When hydraulic pressure greater than osmotic pressure (P > π) is applied to saline feed water, pure water molecules are forced backward through a semi-permeable membrane, leaving concentrated salts behind.

            Membrane Rejection

            Porous polymer thin-film (cellulose acetate / polyamide) blocks 95–99% of dissolved ions, silica, bacteria, and pyrogens.

            Advantages & Limits

            Delivers near-distilled quality without chemical regenerants. Membranes require pre-filtration to prevent chlorine attack and scaling.

            Reverse Osmosis is a pressure-driven membrane process. Explain the three regimes: 1) P < π is natural osmosis where water moves dilute to concentrated; 2) P = π is osmotic equilibrium with zero net flow; 3) P > π (15–40 bar) forces water backward from concentrated to dilute. Highlight membrane materials: cellulose acetate (hydrolyzes above 35°C, limited pH) and polyamide (intolerant of free chlorine). Emphasize that RO rejects bacteria, viruses, pyrogens, and 99% of mineral salts.

            Boiler Operational Troubles

            Priming & foaming

            When feed water quality is neglected, water droplets get dragged into the steam header ('carry-over'). This produces wet steam that erodes turbine blades and reduces efficiency.

              Priming (Mechanical Surge)

              Violent boiling surges drag water droplets into the steam pipe. Prevented with anti-priming steam purifiers and gradual valve operation.

              Foaming (Chemical Froth)

              Oily grease and alkalis form persistent bubble froth on the water surface. Collapsed by castor oil anti-foam drops and boiler blow-down.

              Distinguish clearly: Priming is mechanical (violent boiling drags droplets); Foaming is chemical (oil or alkalis lower surface tension and create stable foam). Both cause carry-over of wet steam. Watch the gauge glass flicker during priming and foaming, and point out how castor oil instantly collapses foam bubbles while blow-down flushes the sludge.
